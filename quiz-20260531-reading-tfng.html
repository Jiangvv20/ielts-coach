📖 阅读文章 · Carbon Markets: The Promise and Perils of Pricing Pollution

As governments search for cost-effective tools to reduce greenhouse gas emissions, carbon markets have emerged as a prominent policy instrument. At their core, these systems assign a monetary price to carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases, creating a financial incentive for businesses to cut their output. The fundamental principle is simple: polluters must either reduce their emissions or purchase permits authorising them to release a set quantity of carbon, while those who emit less than their permitted allocation can sell their surplus credits to others. Proponents argue that harnessing market forces in this way delivers emissions reductions at the lowest possible economic cost.

The world's largest and oldest mandatory carbon market is the European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), which was launched in 2005. It covers approximately 40% of total EU greenhouse gas emissions, primarily from power stations, heavy industry, and aviation within the bloc. Under the system, the total number of permits — known as allowances — is capped and reduced each year, with the ceiling set to fall to 62% below 2005 levels by 2030. Companies unable to reduce their emissions cost-effectively may purchase allowances from more efficient rivals, ensuring that cuts happen wherever they are cheapest. Critics noted, however, that during the system's early years an oversupply of permits kept prices too low to drive meaningful investment in low-carbon technology.

Alongside mandatory compliance markets such as the EU ETS, voluntary carbon markets allow businesses and individuals to offset their emissions by purchasing credits generated by projects that absorb or avoid greenhouse gas emissions. These projects span a wide range of activities: planting trees, building renewable energy facilities in developing countries, and distributing clean cooking stoves that reduce reliance on charcoal. Voluntary carbon credits are not subject to the same strict regulatory oversight as compliance allowances, leading to considerable variation in their quality and credibility. Several investigations have found that some forest offset projects failed to deliver the promised carbon savings.

A persistent challenge for carbon markets is the question of permanence. Carbon stored in forests can be released if the trees are later harvested, burned in wildfires, or destroyed by disease. Unlike CO₂ locked in deep geological formations, forest carbon is inherently reversible. This vulnerability became painfully apparent in 2021, when large wildfires in Oregon burned forests that had been generating carbon credits sold to companies claiming carbon-neutral status. Critics argue that until markets develop robust mechanisms to account for such reversals, forest offsets cannot reliably substitute for genuine emissions reductions at source.

A second fundamental concern is additionality — the requirement that a carbon credit only has value if the emissions reduction it represents would not have occurred anyway. If a company receives credits for preserving a forest that was never at serious risk of being cleared, the offset achieves nothing climatically. Research published in 2023 estimated that a substantial majority of forest credits certified by one leading standards body failed the additionality test. Such findings have intensified calls for much stricter certification requirements and robust independent verification of all carbon projects before credits are issued.

Despite these weaknesses, many economists and policymakers maintain that well-designed carbon pricing remains among the most efficient tools available for driving decarbonisation. The key reforms widely identified include stronger regulatory oversight, more rigorous monitoring and reporting, and a carbon price that is sufficiently high and stable to shift investment decisions at the scale needed. Several nations are expanding domestic carbon pricing mechanisms, and Article 6 of the Paris Agreement provides a framework for linking national markets internationally. Whether carbon markets can evolve quickly enough to contribute decisively to limiting global warming to 1.5°C remains fiercely debated.
