Rethinking Assessment: What Tests Can and Cannot Measure

For most of the twentieth century, standardised testing occupied a central role in education systems worldwide. Proponents argued that objective, uniformly scored assessments provided the fairest means of comparing student achievement across schools and districts, thereby enabling governments to identify underperforming institutions and allocate resources more equitably. Yet by the early twenty-first century, a growing body of empirical research had begun to reveal the considerable limitations of this model, prompting policymakers, educators, and researchers to question whether the dominance of standardised tests was helping or hindering educational progress.

One of the most frequently cited criticisms concerns the narrowing of the curriculum. When schools are evaluated principally on the basis of test scores, teachers face intense pressure to prioritise tested subjects — typically mathematics and literacy — at the expense of arts, physical education, and social-emotional learning. Studies conducted in the United States following the introduction of No Child Left Behind legislation found a measurable decline in instructional time devoted to science, history, and creative subjects, a phenomenon widely known as "teaching to the test." Critics contend that this approach cultivates convergent thinking while suppressing the divergent thinking associated with innovation and problem-solving.

A second concern relates to equity. Standardised tests have consistently been shown to reflect the cultural knowledge and linguistic conventions familiar to affluent, majority-culture students. Children from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, English-language learners, and students with certain learning differences tend to perform less well on standardised instruments — not because they lack ability, but because the tests themselves fail to capture the full range of their competencies. Researchers use the term construct validity to describe the degree to which a test actually measures what it claims to measure; by this standard, many high-stakes examinations are found wanting.

In response, a growing number of institutions have embraced alternative assessment models. Portfolio assessment, in which students curate a collection of their best work over time, captures development and creativity in ways that a single examination cannot. Competency-based education allows students to advance once they have demonstrated mastery of a skill, irrespective of time spent in class. Meanwhile, several prestigious universities in North America have adopted test-optional admissions policies, citing evidence that high school grade point averages are more reliable predictors of first-year performance than standardised entrance examinations.

None of this implies that all formal assessment is without value. Diagnostic assessments, when used formatively to identify knowledge gaps rather than to rank and sort students, can guide teaching with remarkable precision. International benchmarking tools such as PISA provide governments with genuinely useful data about broad trends in literacy and numeracy. The challenge for education systems in the coming decades is to harness the analytical power of assessment while ensuring that the methods employed are equitable, broad enough to capture the full spectrum of human ability, and embedded within a culture of learning rather than a culture of performativity.
