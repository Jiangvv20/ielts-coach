📖 阅读文章 · Neuroscience-Informed Teaching

Neuroscience-informed teaching — the application of brain research to classroom practice — has moved from the fringes of educational debate to mainstream professional development programmes over the past two decades. Yet significant misunderstandings persist, and the gap between what neuroscientific findings actually demonstrate and what teachers believe they demonstrate remains surprisingly wide.

One of the most robustly supported findings is the benefit of spaced practice. When learners revisit material at gradually increasing intervals rather than in a single intensive session, the brain consolidates information far more durably. A 2022 analysis of over two hundred studies confirmed that the spacing effect is one of the largest and most consistent phenomena in memory research, outperforming massed practice by a wide margin. Despite this, students almost universally default to cramming — intensive revision concentrated immediately before an examination — a strategy that produces short-term recall at the expense of lasting retention.

Equally well-established is the superiority of retrieval practice over passive review. Testing oneself on material, even before it has been formally studied, strengthens memory traces more effectively than re-reading the same content repeatedly. Roediger and Karpicke's landmark 2006 experiment showed that students who took three practice tests retained significantly more information one week later than students who had studied the same material three times. Critically, the testing group initially felt less confident than the re-reading group, illustrating that subjective ease of learning is a poor guide to actual retention — a phenomenon known as the fluency illusion.

A third area of neuroeducational research concerns the role of emotion in learning. The amygdala, a brain structure heavily involved in emotional processing, has dense connections to the hippocampus, the region primarily responsible for memory consolidation. Events associated with moderate positive or negative emotion are typically encoded more vividly than emotionally neutral information. Crucially, however, high levels of stress trigger cortisol release, which actively impairs hippocampal function. This suggests that classrooms characterised by punitive discipline, fear of failure, or chronic competitive pressure may paradoxically undermine the very cognitive processes they aim to sharpen.

One popular but empirically unfounded idea that neuroscience has struggled to displace is the notion of fixed learning styles — the belief that individuals have a dominant sensory modality (visual, auditory, or kinesthetic) through which they learn best. Despite decades of research, no credible study has demonstrated that matching instruction to a learner's preferred style produces better outcomes. A 2018 systematic review in Psychological Science concluded that learning-styles theory lacks empirical support and that continuing to promote it in teacher training represents a misallocation of limited professional development time. Teachers are better advised to vary their instructional modalities not because students have fixed preferences, but because multi-modal input enriches encoding for all learners.

The implications for educators are practical. Lesson sequencing should incorporate regular, low-stakes retrieval activities — brief quizzes, pair-share recall tasks, or exit tickets — distributed across the curriculum rather than concentrated at revision time. Classroom culture should prioritise psychological safety over performance pressure, allowing errors to be welcomed as diagnostic information rather than treated as failures deserving sanction. And professional development programmes should assist teachers in distinguishing neuromyths — such as the hemisphere dominance theory that claims people are either left-brained or right-brained — from findings with genuine classroom applicability.
