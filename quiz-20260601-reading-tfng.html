📖 阅读文章 · Freshwater Under Pressure: The Global Water Scarcity Crisis

Of all the water on Earth, only about 2.5 per cent is fresh, and the vast majority of even this small fraction is locked in glaciers, polar ice caps, and deep underground aquifers. As a result, the supply of freshwater that is practically accessible to human populations at any given time amounts to a tiny and unevenly distributed resource. Despite this inherent scarcity, global consumption of freshwater has more than tripled since the middle of the twentieth century, driven by population growth, agricultural expansion, and rising living standards. Water stress — a condition in which demand approaches or exceeds the available supply — now affects roughly two billion people worldwide.

Agriculture accounts for approximately seventy per cent of all freshwater withdrawals globally, dwarfing industrial and domestic use combined. Irrigated farming has been instrumental in feeding a growing world population, but it has come at a significant environmental cost. In many regions, rivers have been diverted so heavily that they no longer reach the sea, and large lake systems have dramatically shrunk. The Aral Sea, once the world's fourth-largest lake, has been reduced to a fraction of its former size following large-scale Soviet irrigation projects in Central Asia. Reversing such damage is technologically possible in theory but requires levels of sustained international cooperation that have rarely been achieved in practice.

Groundwater provides drinking water for roughly half the world's population and irrigates about forty per cent of its farmland. Yet many of the world's most important aquifer systems are being depleted at rates that far outpace their natural recharge. The Ogallala Aquifer beneath the Great Plains of the United States, which supplies water to one of the world's most productive agricultural regions, is being drawn down at a rate approximately ten times faster than it is replenished by rainfall. Once exhausted, such ancient groundwater reserves cannot be restored on any timescale relevant to human civilisation. Scientists warn that the depletion of aquifers represents a slow-moving crisis that will eventually undermine food security across multiple continents.

Climate change is compounding the challenge significantly. Warmer temperatures accelerate evaporation from reservoirs and soils, while altered precipitation patterns are shifting where and when rain falls, often delivering more intense storms to some regions while extending droughts in others. Regions that have historically relied on glacier meltwater to sustain rivers through dry seasons — including parts of South Asia, the Andes, and Central Asia — face the prospect of severely reduced flows as glaciers retreat. The irony is that the countries most dependent on glacial freshwater tend to be among those least responsible for the greenhouse gas emissions that are accelerating glacial loss.

Technological solutions such as seawater desalination and advanced wastewater recycling offer partial answers. Desalination plants now supply a substantial proportion of the drinking water in several Gulf states and in parts of southern Spain and Australia. However, the process is highly energy-intensive, typically requiring substantial quantities of fossil fuels unless powered by renewables, and it generates large volumes of concentrated brine that must be disposed of without harming coastal marine ecosystems. Wastewater recycling — already practised at scale in Singapore and Israel — is generally more energy-efficient than desalination, though it faces cultural resistance in many societies where the concept of drinking reclaimed water remains psychologically uncomfortable.

Ultimately, addressing water scarcity requires a combination of demand reduction, technological innovation, and international governance reform. Pricing water more accurately to reflect its true scarcity value — rather than subsidising it heavily in agriculture — is consistently identified by economists as the single most effective lever available. Yet in practice, water pricing reforms face intense political resistance, particularly from powerful agricultural constituencies. Multilateral frameworks for managing shared river basins and transboundary aquifers remain underdeveloped, even as population growth and climate change make cooperation ever more urgent. Without a fundamental rethinking of how societies value and manage their most essential resource, water scarcity will intensify from a regional challenge into a global emergency.
