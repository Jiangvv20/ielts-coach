📖 阅读文章 · The Microplastic Crisis: A Threat from Shore to Shore

Plastic production has grown exponentially since the mid-twentieth century. More than 400 million tonnes of plastic are manufactured globally each year, yet recycling systems capture only a fraction of this material. The rest is incinerated, deposited in landfill sites, or — most critically — released into the natural environment. Scientists now estimate that between eight and twelve million tonnes of plastic enter the world's oceans annually, where it accumulates across every ocean basin, from the sun-warmed surface of the tropics to the sediments of the deep sea.

Plastic reaches the ocean through multiple pathways. Rivers serve as the primary conduit, transporting waste from inland communities directly to the coast. A 2017 study estimated that ten rivers alone — eight of them in Asia — were responsible for over 90% of plastic delivered to the sea via waterways. Storm drains, coastal tourism, and inadequate waste infrastructure also contribute significantly. Once plastic enters the marine environment, it rarely disappears: instead, ultraviolet radiation and wave action fragment it into progressively smaller pieces.

These fragments — known as microplastics when measuring less than five millimetres — now permeate the marine environment with extraordinary thoroughness. Microplastics exist in two distinct forms. Secondary microplastics arise from the breakdown of larger items such as bottles, bags, and fishing nets. Primary microplastics, by contrast, are manufactured at microscopic scale from the outset, entering waterways through the rinsing of synthetic textiles, the washing of vehicles, and the use of personal care products containing micro-beads. Some studies estimate that a single wash of a synthetic fleece jacket can release hundreds of thousands of fibres into wastewater systems.

The ecological consequences are severe. Marine mammals, seabirds, and fish routinely ingest microplastics, sometimes mistaking them for food. Ingestion blocks digestive systems and reduces appetite, with research indicating that affected individuals consume less food and are less likely to reproduce successfully. Perhaps more alarming is the process of bioaccumulation: as smaller organisms consume microplastics and are in turn eaten by larger predators, concentrations of plastic particles — and the toxic chemical additives attached to their surfaces — magnify at each level of the food chain. Studies on North Sea fish have found measurable microplastic contamination in a significant proportion of species examined.

The implications for human health are increasingly difficult to ignore. Researchers have detected microplastic particles in human blood, breast milk, placentas, and lung tissue. While the long-term health consequences remain uncertain, there is growing concern about the potential for endocrine disruption caused by certain plastic additives, including phthalates and bisphenol A (BPA). Some laboratory studies have linked these compounds to hormonal irregularities and developmental problems, though experts caution that establishing direct causation in human populations requires further epidemiological research.

The international response has been fragmented. In 2019, 187 nations amended the Basel Convention to restrict the export of hazardous plastic waste to developing countries. Dozens of governments have moved to ban single-use plastic items such as bags, straws, and cutlery. Corporate pledges to use recycled content and reduce packaging have multiplied, though critics argue that voluntary commitments without enforceable targets achieve little. A global plastics treaty, under negotiation since 2022, aims to establish binding production caps and extended producer responsibility — but progress has been slowed by disagreement between oil-producing nations and environmental advocates over whether the treaty should address production volumes or focus solely on waste management.

Scientists and campaigners broadly agree that reducing plastic production itself — not merely improving recycling — is the most effective long-term solution. Recycling rates for plastics globally average around 9%, and even well-functioning systems cannot prevent widespread leakage. In the meantime, novel solutions are being explored: researchers have identified bacteria capable of breaking down certain plastic polymers, and engineers are experimenting with biodegradable alternatives derived from seaweed and agricultural by-products. Whether these innovations can scale sufficiently to address a crisis of this magnitude remains, for now, an open question.
