📚 阅读材料 — Urban Heat Islands and Green Infrastructure (约 620 词)

Cities across the world are warming faster than their rural surroundings, a phenomenon known as the urban heat island (UHI) effect. The UHI effect arises because dense concentrations of buildings, roads, and impervious surfaces absorb and retain solar radiation far more efficiently than natural landscapes do. Concrete and asphalt, which blanket most city centres, have a low albedo — meaning they reflect little sunlight — and release stored heat slowly after dark, keeping overnight temperatures elevated. Studies consistently show that urban cores can be 3–8°C warmer than adjacent rural areas, with the gap widening on still, cloudless nights.

The consequences for public health are severe. Prolonged exposure to elevated urban temperatures exacerbates heat-related illnesses, particularly among the elderly, young children, and outdoor workers. Research published in leading medical journals suggests that UHI conditions can double or even triple heat-related mortality during summer heatwaves compared with rural populations exposed to the same ambient temperatures. Hospitals in densely built cities often see patient admissions for cardiovascular and respiratory conditions surge during such events. Paradoxically, the air-conditioning units installed to counter the heat discharge thermal pollution into the surrounding air, amplifying the very problem they are meant to solve.

Green infrastructure — a broad term encompassing street trees, green roofs, urban parks, wetlands, and permeable pavements — is increasingly recognised as a cost-effective countermeasure. Trees cool the air through transpiration and by providing shade that blocks direct solar radiation. A study conducted in Melbourne, Australia, found that a mature street tree can reduce surface temperatures on pavements below its canopy by up to 15°C on a hot day. Green roofs, which replace conventional waterproofed membranes with growing media and vegetation, reduce stormwater runoff, improve insulation, and lower rooftop temperatures by 20–40°C compared with traditional roofs. Critically, these effects accumulate: modelling suggests that increasing urban tree cover by 10% across a typical mid-latitude city could reduce mean summer temperatures by approximately 0.5–0.8°C.

Despite this evidence, urban greening faces significant barriers. Land values in city centres make park expansion prohibitively expensive, and landlords are often reluctant to bear the upfront costs of retrofitting green roofs. Planning regulations in many jurisdictions still favour speed of development over ecological considerations, allowing impervious surface coverage to expand unchecked. Critics also point out that the benefits of green infrastructure are not distributed equitably: wealthier districts tend to have more trees and parks than deprived neighbourhoods, meaning that lower-income residents — who are statistically more likely to live in heat-exposed environments and to lack air-conditioning — bear the brunt of the UHI effect disproportionately.

Proponents of urban greening argue that the economic case is compelling. When ecosystem services such as stormwater management, air purification, and reduced energy demand for cooling are factored in, the financial returns on investment in green infrastructure frequently exceed those of purely grey engineering solutions such as white-painted rooftops or mechanical ventilation systems. Singapore's extensive network of vertical gardens, sky terraces, and roof parks has become an internationally cited example of a city that has embedded nature into its built environment at scale. Singapore's government mandates a minimum Greenery Provision Ratio for new developments, ensuring that green space lost to construction is replaced above ground. Whether such regulatory innovation can be replicated in cities with weaker governance remains an open question, but the pressure to act is mounting as summer temperatures continue to break records worldwide.
