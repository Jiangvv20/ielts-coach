Passage 节选 · The Welfare State in the Twenty-First Century

The welfare state, once celebrated as one of the twentieth century's defining achievements, faces mounting pressures in an era of economic transformation. The proliferation of precarious employment — short-term contracts, zero-hours arrangements, and gig-economy work — has eroded the contributory basis on which most social insurance systems were built. Workers who lack stable, long-term employment records often fall outside eligibility criteria, leaving them without meaningful protection precisely when they are most vulnerable.

Demographic change compounds the fiscal challenge. As populations age across the developed world, the ratio of working-age contributors to retired beneficiaries continues to shift. The OECD has projected that public pension spending could double in some member states by 2060 without structural reform, placing enormous pressure on government budgets already strained by sluggish productivity growth and rising healthcare costs.

Perhaps the most contested debate concerns the design of income support itself. Traditional means-tested benefits have long been criticised for creating a poverty trap: as recipients increase their earnings, benefit withdrawal rates can make additional work financially unrewarding. A growing body of researchers argues that a universal basic income — a flat, unconditional payment to every citizen regardless of employment status — could eliminate this disincentive. Pilot programmes in Finland, Kenya, and Stockton, California have reported promising results, including improved mental health outcomes and no significant reduction in labour market participation.

Housing presents a parallel set of difficulties. In many cities, a shortage of affordable homes has placed enormous strain on low-income households, with rents consuming an ever-larger share of take-home pay. Some commentators attribute this crisis partly to the deregulation of financial markets during the 1980s, which encouraged speculative investment in residential property. Others point to inadequate planning frameworks that restrict new construction and keep housing supply artificially constrained.

Urban regeneration programmes have attempted to address concentrated deprivation in neglected neighbourhoods by attracting private investment and improving public infrastructure. Critics warn, however, that rising property values in the wake of such initiatives can trigger gentrification, displacing the lower-income communities the programmes were intended to help. Robust tenancy protections and social housing commitments are increasingly seen as essential safeguards against this risk.

Ultimately, the future shape of the welfare state reflects political choices about solidarity and collective responsibility. The evidence increasingly suggests that well-designed safety nets — far from creating dependency — are associated with lower crime rates, better public health outcomes, and higher long-run productivity. The challenge for policymakers is to modernise delivery mechanisms, close eligibility gaps left by labour-market change, and sustain public legitimacy for redistribution at a time when trust in institutions is fragile.
