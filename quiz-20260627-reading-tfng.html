Passage · The Automation Paradox: Jobs Lost, Jobs Transformed

When industrial robots began rolling off assembly lines in large numbers during the 1980s, economists and trade unions alike predicted a wave of unemployment that would hollow out the manufacturing workforce. That wave arrived, but it did not arrive alone: alongside job destruction came job creation, as new industries absorbed millions of displaced workers into roles that had not existed a decade earlier. This recurring pattern — technological disruption followed by labour market adaptation — is now at the centre of an urgent and unresolved debate about artificial intelligence and the future of work.

Unlike previous waves of automation, which primarily replaced manual and routine cognitive tasks, AI systems are encroaching on activities long considered the exclusive preserve of educated professionals. Radiology algorithms now outperform junior doctors at detecting certain tumours; contract-review software can process thousands of legal documents in the time it would take a paralegal team weeks to complete. The International Labour Organisation estimates that roughly 14 percent of jobs in OECD countries face a high risk of significant automation within the next two decades, while a further 32 percent will undergo substantial changes in how tasks are performed.

Proponents of technological optimism insist that such figures are deceptive. They point to the "lump of labour" fallacy — the mistaken belief that there is a fixed amount of work to be done in an economy, so that each job lost to a machine permanently reduces employment. In practice, they argue, productivity gains lower prices, stimulate demand, and generate new categories of employment. The emergence of social media, for instance, created entirely new professions — content creator, data analyst, UX designer — that were inconceivable to workers in the 1990s.

Sceptics, however, caution that the pace of change this time may outstrip the economy's capacity to adapt. Workers displaced from routine manufacturing jobs in the twentieth century could, with moderate retraining, find equivalent roles in service industries. Retraining a middle-aged accountant whose entire career rested on skills that AI now replicates cheaply is a far more formidable challenge. There is also a distributional concern: the gains from automation tend to flow disproportionately to capital owners and highly skilled workers, widening income inequality even if aggregate employment remains stable.

Governments are responding with a patchwork of policies. Some countries are experimenting with portable skills accounts — subsidised training vouchers that workers can use at any point in their careers rather than only when between jobs. Others are exploring shorter working weeks, arguing that sharing available work more broadly is preferable to concentrating income among those who retain employment. A handful of jurisdictions have floated the idea of a robot tax — a levy on firms that replace human workers with automated systems — though critics argue this would simply push innovation offshore. What remains clear is that labour market institutions designed in the twentieth century for a world of stable, long-term employment will need fundamental redesign if they are to protect workers navigating an economy shaped by intelligent machines.

Q01 · T/F/NG
According to the passage, economists unanimously predicted that industrial robots in the 1980s would lead to permanent and irreversible mass unemployment.
B · False
原文: "economists and trade unions alike predicted a wave of unemployment" ——预测的是失业浪潮, 并非说"永久且不可逆"; 且原文紧接着说"alongside job destruction came job creation", 说明就业是有恢复的。"unanimously" 也是原文没有的信息, 且结果并非不可逆。
