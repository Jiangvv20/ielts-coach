Blue Carbon: Coastal Ecosystems and the Battle Against Climate Change

ABeneath the surface of the world's shallow coastal waters lies an often overlooked climate weapon: the blue carbon ecosystem. Seagrass meadows, mangrove forests, and tidal salt marshes collectively cover only around 0.2 percent of the ocean floor, yet they are among the most carbon-dense habitats on Earth. Unlike the carbon stored in terrestrial forests — which resides predominantly in above-ground biomass — coastal blue carbon is buried in waterlogged, oxygen-poor sediments, where microbial decomposition is severely inhibited. This anaerobic environment allows organic matter to accumulate for millennia, with carbon buried in mangrove soils sometimes exceeding five thousand years in age. In per-hectare terms, blue carbon ecosystems sequester carbon at rates up to five times greater than those of mature tropical forests.

BThe climate significance of coastal ecosystems extends beyond their capacity to absorb carbon dioxide. Mangroves and seagrasses also act as nurseries for juvenile fish, filter agricultural runoff before it reaches the open ocean, stabilise shorelines against wave erosion, and protect coastal communities from storm surges. Research published in Nature Climate Change estimated that mangroves alone prevent storm damage valued at approximately $65 billion annually through their role as natural coastal buffers. This multi-functionality — providing simultaneous ecological and economic services — is increasingly cited by conservation economists as making blue carbon habitats among the highest-value natural assets in the world.

CDespite their importance, blue carbon ecosystems have been devastated at alarming rates. Globally, up to 50 percent of tidal wetlands, 35 percent of mangrove forests, and 29 percent of seagrass meadows have been lost since the mid-twentieth century, primarily driven by coastal development, aquaculture expansion, and pollution. The consequences for climate are severe: when blue carbon ecosystems are disturbed or destroyed, the ancient carbon stored in their sediments is exposed to oxygen and rapidly oxidised, releasing greenhouse gases that may have been safely locked away for thousands of years. A 2017 study estimated that the degradation of coastal wetlands releases between 0.15 and 1.02 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide annually — a figure comparable to the emissions of some medium-sized industrialised nations.

DRecognition of blue carbon's potential has sparked growing interest in including coastal ecosystems within national climate commitments. Several countries, including Australia, Indonesia, and Madagascar, have begun incorporating blue carbon into their Nationally Determined Contributions under the Paris Agreement, effectively counting mangrove restoration and protection as equivalent to emissions reductions. Proponents argue that this approach is not only environmentally sound but also economically attractive: the cost of restoring a hectare of mangrove forest is typically a fraction of the cost of implementing equivalent emissions reductions through industrial decarbonisation. Critics, however, caution that carbon accounting for blue carbon is technically complex, since accurate measurement of sediment carbon stocks requires specialised sampling techniques and is subject to high levels of uncertainty.

ECommunity-based conservation has emerged as a particularly promising model for blue carbon protection. In the Philippines, local fishing communities that historically relied on mangrove forests for both food and coastal protection have been trained to monitor and restore coastal habitats in exchange for formally recognised rights over the areas they steward. Early evaluations of these programmes suggest that community-led management achieves comparable conservation outcomes to government-administered protected areas at significantly lower cost. The dual benefit — protecting both livelihoods and ecosystems — has made this model attractive to international donors and development agencies seeking cost-effective climate solutions.

FLooking ahead, scientists argue that blue carbon alone cannot solve the climate crisis, but that its integration into comprehensive climate strategies could yield disproportionate benefits relative to the investment required. The challenge lies in overcoming the institutional inertia that has historically left coastal ecosystems outside mainstream climate policy. As awareness grows of the carbon and biodiversity value embedded in what were once dismissed as "unproductive" wetlands, there are cautious grounds for optimism that these habitats will at last receive the protection — and the resources — they deserve.
