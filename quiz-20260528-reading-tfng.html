📖 阅读文章 · Rewilding: Returning Nature to its Former Self

Rewilding — the large-scale restoration of natural ecosystems through the reintroduction of species that have been lost from an area — has emerged as one of the most ambitious conservation strategies of the twenty-first century. Unlike traditional conservation, which typically focuses on managing human-altered landscapes to prevent further degradation, rewilding takes a more radical stance: allowing nature to reassert itself and, where necessary, actively reintroducing species that once played critical ecological roles.

The conceptual foundation of rewilding rests on the idea of trophic cascades — the chain reactions that ripple through an entire ecosystem when a top predator is removed or returned. The reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in 1995 has become the most widely cited illustration of this principle. Within years of the wolves' return, elk populations were kept in check, allowing overgrazed riverbank vegetation to regenerate. The restored plant cover stabilised riverbanks, reduced erosion, altered stream courses, and ultimately changed the physical geography of the park — a sequence of events popularly described as wolves 'changing rivers'. Critics note, however, that isolating the wolves' contribution from other concurrent factors — such as drought cycles and natural shifts in elk behaviour — is methodologically complex. Some ecologists caution against drawing sweeping conclusions from a single case study.

In Europe, rewilding projects have multiplied significantly since the founding of Rewilding Europe in 2011. The organisation has facilitated the return of European bison, Eurasian lynx, Eurasian beaver, and white-tailed eagles to fragmented landscapes across the continent. Beavers in particular have attracted considerable attention for their ecosystem-engineering capabilities: their dams slow water flow, create wetland habitats, and dramatically reduce downstream flooding. A decade-long study conducted in Devon, England, found that streams with reintroduced beavers had significantly lower peak flow rates following heavy rainfall compared with control streams — services that would otherwise require costly human-built infrastructure.

Rewilding also intersects powerfully with climate policy. Intact ecosystems — particularly peatlands, mangroves, and old-growth forests — store disproportionately large amounts of carbon relative to their area. Allowing degraded peatlands to rewet, for instance, could restore their function as carbon sinks. The UK Committee on Climate Change has estimated that restoring damaged upland peat could sequester up to 40 million tonnes of CO₂ equivalent over the coming fifty years.

Not all perspectives on rewilding are enthusiastic, however. Farmers and rural communities have raised legitimate concerns about the return of large predators, citing risks to livestock and livelihoods. In Norway, the reintroduction of wolves provoked intense public debate and periodic protests from farming communities. Conservation scientists acknowledge that the social and political dimensions of rewilding — particularly in densely populated Europe — are just as challenging as the ecological ones. Projects that fail to secure community buy-in, they warn, risk being reversed by public pressure.

Proponents of rewilding ultimately argue that it represents not the abandonment of human responsibility for nature, but a recognition that the most resilient and cost-effective form of conservation may be to allow natural processes, once freed from excessive interference, to drive the restoration work themselves.
