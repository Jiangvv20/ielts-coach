📚 Reading Passage · ~620 words

The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence across virtually every sector of the global economy has prompted an urgent conversation among policymakers, technologists and civil society groups. What was once a subject confined to computer science laboratories is now reshaping labour markets, healthcare systems, criminal justice and democratic institutions. Yet despite the scale of this transformation, coherent and enforceable regulatory frameworks remain elusive in most parts of the world.

The European Union has moved furthest and fastest. Its landmark AI Act, adopted in 2024, classifies AI systems by risk level and imposes strict obligations on those deployed in high-stakes settings such as biometric surveillance, credit scoring and medical diagnosis. Developers of high-risk systems must conduct comprehensive risk assessments, maintain detailed technical documentation and guarantee a meaningful degree of human oversight. Penalties for non-compliance can reach up to €30 million or 6% of a company's global annual turnover — whichever is greater.

The United States has taken a markedly different approach, opting for sector-specific guidance rather than overarching legislation. Federal agencies, from the Food and Drug Administration to the Federal Trade Commission, have issued guidance for AI within their respective domains, but critics argue that this patchwork of rules fails to address systemic risks that cut across industry boundaries. The Biden administration's 2023 Executive Order on AI established voluntary commitments from leading developers regarding safety testing, but these lack the force of law and have yet to be fully codified.

Meanwhile, authoritarian governments have turned to artificial intelligence primarily as an instrument of social control. Facial recognition technology, predictive policing algorithms and social credit systems have been deployed extensively in several countries, raising profound questions about the compatibility of algorithmic governance with fundamental human rights. Critics of these systems point to significant evidence of algorithmic bias, as facial recognition models have been shown to misidentify individuals from certain ethnic groups at rates far exceeding those observed for majority populations.

The debate over who should bear responsibility for AI-generated harm remains unresolved. When an autonomous vehicle causes a fatal accident, or when a credit-scoring algorithm wrongly denies a loan, the question of liability is far from straightforward. Establishing clear legal standards for tech accountability is considered by many experts to be the single most pressing challenge in digital policy today.

Alongside these concerns, advocates for innovation caution against regulatory overreach. Excessive or poorly designed rules, they argue, risk driving investment and talent towards jurisdictions with lighter oversight — a dynamic known as jurisdictional arbitrage. Striking the right balance between protecting the public and preserving the conditions that allow transformative technologies to flourish requires not only legal expertise but also a deep technical understanding that many legislative bodies currently lack.

One area of emerging consensus is the importance of algorithmic transparency. If individuals are to understand and challenge decisions made about them by automated systems, those systems must be explainable. Some jurisdictions are already mandating that individuals be informed when automated decision-making affects them significantly and be given the right to request a human review. Whether this approach can be reconciled with the commercial imperative to keep proprietary algorithms confidential is a question that courts and regulators will be grappling with for years to come.
