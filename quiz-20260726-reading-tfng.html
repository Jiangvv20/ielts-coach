📚 阅读材料 — The Global Carbon Market: Promise, Perils, and the Path to Net Zero (约 610 词)

Carbon pricing is widely regarded by economists as one of the most efficient tools for reducing greenhouse gas emissions. By assigning a monetary cost to each tonne of carbon dioxide released into the atmosphere, it creates a direct financial incentive for companies and governments to decarbonise. Two main mechanisms have emerged globally: carbon taxes, which set a fixed price per tonne of emissions, and emissions trading schemes (ETS), which cap total emissions and allow participants to buy and sell permits within that cap. Both approaches fall under the broader umbrella of the global carbon market, a system that has grown rapidly since the signing of the Kyoto Protocol in 1997 but remains deeply fragmented and, critics argue, far from sufficient.

The European Union Emissions Trading System (EU ETS), launched in 2005, is the world's largest carbon market by value, covering roughly 40% of the EU's total greenhouse gas emissions across power generation, heavy industry, and aviation. Carbon prices under the EU ETS have risen dramatically in recent years, reaching over €80 per tonne by 2024 — a level many economists consider approaching the threshold needed to shift investment meaningfully toward clean alternatives. However, the EU ETS was for many years criticised for allocating too many free permits to industry, effectively suppressing the carbon price and weakening the incentive to reduce emissions. Reforms introduced under the Fit for 55 package — which commits the EU to cutting net emissions by 55% by 2030 — have progressively tightened the cap and reduced free allowances.

Beyond Europe, carbon markets vary enormously in scope and stringency. China launched the world's largest carbon market by volume in 2021, initially covering only the power sector, with ambitions to extend to other industries in subsequent phases. The Chinese market has faced criticism for setting relatively low price benchmarks and for relying heavily on self-reported emissions data, raising concerns about verification reliability. Meanwhile, California's cap-and-trade programme has been operating since 2013 and is widely considered a model for regulatory robustness, though its carbon prices remain well below European levels.

A parallel and more controversial system operates through voluntary carbon markets (VCMs), where corporations purchase carbon credits generated by projects such as reforestation, methane capture, and renewable energy installation to offset their residual emissions. VCMs have grown explosively, driven by net-zero pledges from multinationals. Yet they have attracted intense scrutiny: investigative journalism and independent research have found that a significant proportion of widely sold credits — particularly those from forest protection projects — deliver far fewer actual emission reductions than claimed, a practice critics label as greenwashing. The integrity of VCMs depends heavily on standards bodies such as Verra and the Gold Standard, which are themselves under pressure to tighten verification protocols.

Proponents argue that even an imperfect carbon market sends an important price signal and mobilises private capital toward low-carbon investment at a scale governments alone cannot match. The International Monetary Fund estimates that a global carbon price of at least $75 per tonne would be needed by 2030 to keep warming below 2°C, yet the vast majority of emissions currently priced globally fall well below that level. The OECD has noted that perverse subsidies for fossil fuels still dwarf investment in carbon pricing, undermining its effectiveness. Until governments demonstrate the political will to close this gap — by raising carbon prices, eliminating fossil fuel subsidies, and establishing credible cross-border carbon border adjustment mechanisms — the carbon market alone is unlikely to deliver the transformative change climate science demands.
