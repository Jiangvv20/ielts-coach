Reading T/F/NG · 包容性教育与神经多样性
Day 59 · 11 题 · ~12 分钟

Passage · 节选 (约 620 词)

For much of the twentieth century, children with learning differences were educated in separate special schools, often with limited resources and low expectations. The rationale was straightforward: specialist environments would better serve those whose needs diverged from the norm. Yet by the 1990s, a powerful counter-movement had gathered momentum. Propelled by the United Nations’ Salamanca Statement of 1994, which called on governments to adopt inclusive education as a default policy, mainstream schools around the world began to open their doors to learners with physical, sensory, and cognitive differences.

Inclusion, however, is far more than a logistical arrangement. Its advocates argue that placing disabled and non-disabled students in the same classroom yields social as well as academic benefits for all parties. Non-disabled peers develop empathy and learn to work with people whose strengths and challenges differ from their own, a preparation for life in a diverse society. For students with disabilities, the academic bar is raised rather than lowered, and the stigma associated with segregated schooling is eliminated. Critics counter that without adequate funding, training, and support staff, inclusion amounts to little more than physical integration: students with complex needs may be present in the room but effectively marginalised within it.

The debate has grown considerably more nuanced since the concept of neurodiversity entered mainstream discourse in the early 2000s. Originally coined by Australian sociologist Judy Singer in 1998, the term reframes conditions such as autism, dyslexia, ADHD, and dyscalculia not as deficits to be remediated but as natural variations in human cognition, each associated with distinctive strengths alongside genuine challenges. Proponents of the neurodiversity movement argue that many of the difficulties faced by neurodivergent individuals are not intrinsic but rather arise from an environment designed exclusively for neurotypical people.

Research into the academic benefits of neurodiversity-informed pedagogy is still emerging, yet early findings are promising. Studies conducted in Finland and Canada between 2018 and 2023 found that classrooms that explicitly adopted universal design for learning (UDL) principles — offering multiple means of representation, action, and engagement — raised achievement for all learners, not merely those with identified needs. Crucially, these gains did not come at the expense of high-achieving students, dismantling a persistent fear among some parents.

The funding question remains contentious. In England, the number of pupils identified as having special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) has risen by nearly 30 percent since 2016, yet the government’s SEND budget has not kept pace. Many local authorities have accumulated significant deficits attempting to fulfil their statutory duties, and a parliamentary review published in 2023 concluded that the system was “in crisis.” Similar pressures exist across much of the developed world, suggesting that the philosophical shift towards inclusion has outrun the practical infrastructure needed to sustain it.

Ultimately, the success of inclusive education depends on a constellation of factors: well-trained and adequately resourced teachers, flexible assessment systems that accommodate different ways of demonstrating knowledge, a school culture that values difference, and sustained political will. Where these conditions are met, the evidence suggests that inclusion is not merely an ethical imperative but an effective educational strategy. Where they are absent, inclusion risks becoming a hollow gesture that serves neither disabled nor non-disabled learners well.
