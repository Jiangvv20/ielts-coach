📚 Reading Passage · ~610 words

The miniaturisation of computing hardware and dramatic advances in sensor technology have combined to produce a generation of wearable devices that promise to reshape healthcare delivery. Smartwatches, biosensing patches and intelligent clothing now continuously collect a broad range of physiological data—heart rate, blood-oxygen saturation, skin temperature, stress indicators and even glucose levels—creating rich streams of biometric information that were once obtainable only in clinical settings. Global shipments of consumer health wearables exceeded 600 million units in 2025, reflecting an appetite for personal monitoring that shows no sign of abating.

The case for wearables rests on three interlocking arguments. First, continuous monitoring enables early detection of anomalies long before overt symptoms appear. Studies have demonstrated that commercially available smartwatches can identify atrial fibrillation—a potentially life-threatening cardiac arrhythmia—with accuracy comparable to clinical electrocardiograms, alerting wearers before they experience any symptoms at all. Second, the telemetry these devices generate allows clinicians to practise predictive medicine: by analysing real-time biometric streams, healthcare professionals can identify patients at elevated risk and intervene before a condition deteriorates. Third, the engagement effect—the tendency of wearers to modify behaviour when confronted with live health metrics—has been associated with measurable improvements in patients managing hypertension and type 2 diabetes.

Critics are not persuaded by this optimism. A fundamental objection concerns data quality: consumer devices are engineered primarily for convenience rather than clinical precision, and their measurements may contain systematic errors that medical professionals cannot reliably interpret. The sheer volume of continuous data generated is itself problematic: healthcare systems in many countries lack the interoperable infrastructure required to ingest, store and analyse such data in a clinically meaningful way. A related concern, sometimes labelled "quantified-self syndrome", describes the tendency of otherwise healthy individuals to develop health anxiety when they monitor their bodies obsessively—a pattern that may paradoxically drive up demand for unnecessary medical consultations and investigations.

Privacy and data governance pose perhaps the most intractable challenges. Wearable companies accumulate extraordinarily intimate personal information, yet in many jurisdictions the legal protections that apply to medical records do not extend to data harvested by consumer devices. This regulatory gap means user data may, in practice, be sold to insurance companies, employers or pharmaceutical firms without the individual's knowledge or meaningful consent. The prospect of differential insurance premiums calculated from continuous biometric monitoring raises profound ethical concerns that legislators have only recently begun to address.

Despite these tensions, clinical adoption of remote patient monitoring is accelerating. Hospital systems in North America and Europe have launched programmes in which recently discharged patients wear biosensing devices transmitting real-time telemetry to clinical teams. Early evidence suggests significant reductions in hospital readmission rates, particularly for patients with chronic cardiac and respiratory conditions. Regulatory agencies including the FDA and the European Medicines Agency have begun approving certain wearables as certified medical devices, subjecting them to clinical-validation standards designed to enhance their reliability and accuracy.

Looking further ahead, the convergence of wearable hardware, low-latency 5G connectivity and artificial intelligence is expected to enable truly predictive and preventive medicine: systems capable of detecting the earliest physiological signatures of disease and triggering clinical intervention at the optimal moment. Whether this vision ultimately transforms healthcare or merely exacerbates existing inequalities—between those who can afford sophisticated devices and those who cannot—will depend critically on decisions being made now about universal access, data sovereignty and the governance of an increasingly medicalised everyday life.

📝 Part B · 词汇填空 — Vocabulary Fill-in (Questions 7–10)

从今日词卡中选词，填入句中空格，注意词形变化。

词库: telemetry · biometric · interoperable · latency · wearable · synchronise

Question 7 of 10
Hospitals are now using remote ________ to track patients' vital signs continuously from their homes after discharge.
Answer: telemetry. "Telemetry" (遥测) refers to the automated transmission of measurements from a remote source — exactly the technology used for remote patient monitoring described in paragraph 5.
Question 8 of 10
Smartwatches collect ________ data such as heart rate, blood-oxygen levels and skin temperature around the clock.
Answer: biometric. "Biometric" (生物特征的) data refers to measurements derived from the body, such as heart rate and temperature — the passage uses this word explicitly in paragraph 1.
Question 9 of 10
Healthcare systems cannot fully exploit wearable data unless their digital platforms are ________, allowing seamless sharing across hospitals and clinics.
Answer: interoperable. "Interoperable" (互操作的) means capable of exchanging and using data across different systems — paragraph 3 uses this word to describe the infrastructure gap in many healthcare systems.
Question 10 of 10
Reducing network ________ to near-zero is essential for wearable sensors used in surgical robotics, where real-time precision is critical.
Answer: latency. "Latency" (延迟) refers to the time delay in data transmission. The final paragraph mentions "low-latency 5G connectivity" as a key enabler for next-generation medical wearables.
