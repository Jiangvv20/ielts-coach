Passage · Culinary Tourism and the Politics of Authenticity (~620 words)

The global rise of culinary tourism — travel motivated primarily by the desire to experience local food cultures — represents one of the most significant transformations in the contemporary travel industry. A 2025 report by the World Food Travel Association estimated that food-related experiences now account for approximately 35% of all tourism expenditure worldwide, a figure that has more than doubled since 2010. Unlike conventional sightseeing, which treats attractions as discrete spectacles to be consumed and departed, culinary tourism places eating at the centre of cultural engagement, positioning local cuisine as the most direct route to understanding a society’s history, geography, and values.

The relationship between food and cultural identity is both profound and contested. Anthropologists have long argued that the recipes, ingredients, and dining rituals of a community constitute a form of living heritage: a set of practices that encode collective memory and transmit cultural knowledge across generations. It was partly in recognition of this that UNESCO, in 2010, established a new category of Intangible Cultural Heritage specifically for culinary traditions, adding entries such as the Gastronomic Meal of the French and the traditional Mexican cuisine of the community culture of Michoácan.

Yet the growing commercialisation of food culture for tourist consumption has generated a parallel anxiety: that the dishes served in tourist-facing restaurants increasingly diverge from authentic local practices. Scholars of food culture have documented a phenomenon they call the “tourist menu effect” — the systematic adaptation of local dishes to conform to the perceived preferences of international visitors. Spices are reduced; textures are altered; presentation is standardised. The result is a sanitised version of a cuisine that pleases tourists but alienates locals who recognise little of their own culinary tradition in what is served.

The economic geography of culinary tourism adds further complexity. As food districts in cities such as Lisbon, Oaxaca, and Penang become internationally celebrated for their cuisine, the resulting surge in tourist footfall frequently triggers a process of gentrification that displaces the very vendors and family restaurants that made the area attractive. Property values rise; rents increase; the small, family-run eateries that originally defined the neighbourhood’s culinary character are replaced by commercial operations better positioned to absorb the costs of prime urban real estate. Paradoxically, the success of authentic culinary tourism often destroys the authenticity it sought to celebrate.

A more optimistic reading focuses on the creative potential of cultural contact through food. Food historians point to the emergence of what they term “third-culture cuisines” — distinctive culinary traditions that develop in diaspora communities and constitute a genuine synthesis of home and host cultures. Japanese-Brazilian sushi, Peruvian-Chinese chifa, and the British Indian curry tradition are examples of hybrid cuisines that, far from representing cultural dilution, embody a dynamic cultural creativity that enriches both source and host societies.

The future trajectory of culinary tourism will likely depend on the choices made by individual travellers. Research published in the journal Food, Culture & Society in 2024 found that tourists who made deliberate efforts to eat in locally-owned restaurants, attend food markets, and participate in cooking workshops reported higher levels of cultural immersion and personal satisfaction than those who relied on hotel restaurants or international food chains. Destination management organisations in countries including Japan and Peru have begun introducing “culinary authenticity pledges” that encourage visitors to seek out locally owned and operated food businesses, partly as an economic measure and partly as a strategy to slow the pace of gentrification.

Q08 · 词汇填空
UNESCO works to protect culinary traditions by listing them as ______ Cultural Heritage, recognising food practices as a form of living cultural knowledge.
✅ 正确答案: intangible
原文第二段: “UNESCO…established a new category of Intangible Cultural Heritage specifically for culinary traditions.” Intangible = 无形的，指非物质文化遗产。
Q09 · 词汇填空
When spices are reduced and textures are altered to meet tourist expectations, scholars argue that the result is a ______ version of the original cuisine.
✅ 正确答案: sanitised
原文第三段: “The result is a sanitised version of a cuisine that pleases tourists but alienates locals.” Sanitised = 经过净化/处理的，此处指被削去地方特色的版本。
Q10 · 词汇填空
When tourism makes a food neighbourhood famous, rising property values and rents often trigger a process of ______ that forces out the original local vendors.
✅ 正确答案: gentrification
原文第四段: “the resulting surge in tourist footfall frequently triggers a process of gentrification that displaces the very vendors.” Gentrification = 绅士化/高档化，指社区因地产升值而排挤原住民或小商户。
Q11 · 词汇填空
Food historians use the term “______ -culture cuisines” to describe hybrid culinary traditions that emerge when diaspora communities blend elements of their home and host cultures.
✅ 正确答案: third
原文第五段: “the emergence of what they term ‘third-culture cuisines’ — distinctive culinary traditions that develop in diaspora communities.”
