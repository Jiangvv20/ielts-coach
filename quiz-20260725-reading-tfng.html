📚 阅读材料 — The Sixth Mass Extinction: Causes, Scale, and Conservation Response (约 630 词)

Earth is currently experiencing what many scientists describe as the sixth mass extinction event in its 4.5-billion-year history. Unlike the five preceding extinction events — which were caused by natural catastrophes such as asteroid impacts or volcanic super-eruptions — the current crisis is driven almost entirely by human activity. Habitat loss resulting from agricultural expansion, urbanisation, and infrastructure development is widely identified as the primary driver, accounting for an estimated 73% of species at risk. Pollution, invasive species, overexploitation through poaching and unsustainable fishing, and climate change compound these pressures, forming what conservation biologists call a "deadly cocktail" of threats from which no ecosystem on Earth is fully insulated.

The scale of the crisis is staggering. The IUCN Red List, which is the world's most authoritative database of species status, currently classifies over 40,000 species as endangered or critically endangered — roughly 28% of all species assessed. Vertebrate populations have declined by an average of 69% since 1970, according to the WWF's Living Planet Index. Insects, which underpin most terrestrial food webs as pollinators and decomposers, are disappearing at rates three times faster than mammals or birds. Scientists estimate that the current extinction rate is between 100 and 1,000 times higher than the natural background rate observed in the fossil record, though precise figures are contested because so many species remain formally undescribed.

Conservationists have responded with a range of strategies. Protected areas, which cover approximately 17% of land and 8% of ocean globally, have demonstrably slowed habitat degradation where enforcement is adequate. The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, agreed in 2022, set an ambitious target of protecting 30% of land and oceans by 2030 — the so-called "30x30" goal. Rewilding initiatives, which aim to restore self-regulating ecosystems by reintroducing keystone species such as wolves, lynx, and beavers, have produced dramatic results in some regions: the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 triggered a trophic cascade that altered vegetation patterns and even reshaped river channels. Captive breeding programmes have rescued several species from the absolute brink of extinction, including the Arabian oryx and the California condor.

Critics, however, argue that conservation efforts remain dangerously underfunded and piecemeal. Protected areas are often poorly enforced "paper parks" that offer wildlife little real protection from poachers and illegal loggers. The 30x30 target has attracted controversy because some proposed conservation zones would displace indigenous communities who have managed their lands sustainably for generations — a practice critics call "fortress conservation." Furthermore, climate change is increasingly forcing species to migrate outside protected area boundaries in search of suitable thermal conditions, rendering static reserve networks of limited value unless habitat corridors connecting them are established.

The economic argument for biodiversity conservation is powerful. Ecosystem services provided by wild species — including pollination, water purification, climate regulation, and disease control — are estimated to be worth trillions of dollars annually. A world with substantially fewer species would be a world that is not only ecologically impoverished but also far more vulnerable to crop failure, disease outbreaks, and economic disruption. Despite this, less than 3% of global GDP is invested in nature protection, compared with over 7% spent annually on subsidies that directly harm biodiversity through incentivising agricultural expansion and fossil fuel consumption. Redirecting even a fraction of these perverse subsidies towards conservation and ecosystem restoration could, economists argue, yield returns many times greater than the cost.
